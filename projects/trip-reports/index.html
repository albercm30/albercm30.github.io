Trip Reports

Wildlife guides spend their evenings writing trip reports by hand. This web app turns a guide's quick notes and photos into a polished report with a checked species list, and the guide approves it before anyone sees it. It is built as a real product, with team accounts and subscriptions, and there is a demo you can try in the browser.

Problem

Birding and wildlife tour companies send their guests a trip report: a daily narrative, a species list and photos. Guides usually write it by hand after a long day in the field, from memory and a scribbled checklist. It takes an hour that nobody has, species names drift between days, and the list is rarely checked against a standard taxonomy.

How it works

The guide spends a few minutes a day on input: notes typed or dictated the way they would tell a colleague, a rough species tally such as "40 griffons, lynx x1!, heard eagle owl, warbler?" and a few photos. Each species name is checked against a standard taxonomy, which also fills in the scientific name, and a drafter writes the day's narrative from the guide's own notes and species list.

The output is a branded report with the operator's logo, colour and sign-off, a day-by-day species table and a photo gallery, in English, Spanish, German or French. Guests open it through a private link the operator can revoke, it prints to PDF, and the sightings export as CSV. Operators can also reuse the season's numbers for marketing.

Three rules

  • It never adds a species on its own. The narrative only mentions animals the guide actually listed. Anything uncertain (a question mark, "maybe", a name that isn't in the taxonomy) is flagged and waits for the guide.
  • The guide approves everything. Nothing reaches a client until the guide has resolved every flag and pressed approve.
  • The operator owns the data. Each approved report is also structured sighting data (species, count, place, day, observer) that is shared only with the operator's permission.

What the demo does

The demo above is the real workflow in miniature, in English and Spanish. A guide can dictate or type a day the way they would tell a colleague, with no format. The tool picks out the days, dates, times (including “half past six” and “a las siete menos cuarto”), weather, places, species and counts, plus behaviour, highlights and guests. It treats “didn't see it” as a miss rather than a sighting, and flags anything uncertain, heard-only or unknown for the guide to resolve. A live panel shows what it understood while the guide speaks.

It then drafts the narrative in three tones, builds the species table in English or Spanish with scientific names, adds the approval step, and exports the sightings as CSV or JSON.

Guide stats

Every approved report can be saved to the guide's logbook. The stats tab turns the logbook into weekly, monthly, yearly and all-time numbers: guiding days, guests, species, individuals, new species, species per day, the best day, how many sightings are backed by a photo, and the share of days on which each species was seen, which is an honest answer to “will we see it?”. A one-page card summarises it for clients and can be printed. The figures are self-reported by the guide, so in the product each one links back to the sightings and photos behind it.

The demo is a separate, browser-only version of the same idea, so it runs for free and uploads nothing: it matches about 390 species instead of checking the full GBIF taxonomy online, understands language with fixed rules, and writes the narrative from templates. Voice dictation uses the browser's own speech recognition where available. The demo's sample history is generated and clearly labelled as such.

The product behind the demo

Beyond the demo there is a working web app for operators, written in Python:

  • Accounts and teams. Sign-up with a free trial, login and password reset, guide invitations with seat limits, and owner and guide roles.
  • Trips and days. Notes, place, date, photos (resized, with location data stripped) and a species list per day, saved as you type. The tally parser turns rough text into rows with counts, highlights, heard-only and doubtful flags.
  • Drafting. A free, rule-based drafter writes each day from the guide's notes and species list only. With an Anthropic API key it uses Claude instead, under strict no-invention rules, a monthly quota per plan and automatic fallback to the free drafter.
  • Approval and reports. A guide approves each day, and the report page shows what is still missing before it is sent.
  • Season insights. Target-species success rates, the best months for each species, website lines ready to copy and a season summary.
  • Billing. Stripe Checkout, the Customer Portal and webhooks keep each company's plan in sync. When a trial ends unpaid the account becomes read-only, with export still available, and a cancellation shows as "Cancelled, ends on [date]" instead of "Renews on [date]".
  • Privacy. Data export, account deletion and privacy and terms page templates. The interface is in English and Spanish.

Business and data

The customer is the operator: a small monthly fee per company, fully remote, with no data licensing problem because the content comes from the operator. The test plans are Starter (€29 a month, 2 guide seats) and Pro (€59 a month, 8 seats), with a yearly price at ten months. The side effect is the interesting part: with permission, every report adds clean, expert-verified sighting records from many countries at once, which is the dataset that trip and chance forecasting is usually missing.

How it is built

  • FastAPI, SQLAlchemy and Alembic with server-rendered Jinja pages and a little vanilla JavaScript. PostgreSQL in production, SQLite for local development.
  • Free hosting. The database is on Neon and the app on Render from a Blueprint file. Photos live in the database, so no file storage is needed. The free web service sleeps when idle, so the first visit after a while takes about 30 seconds.
  • GBIF's backbone taxonomy for species names, which has a licence that allows this use.
  • The Claude API is optional. The product works fully without it, and the narrative is only ever allowed to mention animals from the matched species list.
  • Stripe in test mode, with signed webhooks, ready to switch to live keys.
  • Security basics. Passwords hashed with scrypt, session cookies, CSRF protection, rate limits, security headers and photo location data removed.
  • 42 automated tests covering accounts, trips, the parser and billing.

Honest limits

This is an early-stage product with no customers yet, and billing runs in Stripe's test mode. The free drafter writes from templates, the species parser will miss unusual phrasing, and the free hosting is slow to wake up. The legal pages are templates that need a professional review before taking real customers. Uploaded photos are only shown in the report and are not analysed. Voice transcription with Whisper and a WhatsApp or Telegram bot are ideas for later and are not built. The demo above is the in-browser version, so what it saves stays in your browser.
