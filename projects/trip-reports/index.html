Trip Reports

Wildlife guides spend their evenings writing trip reports by hand. This web app turns a guide's quick notes, voice notes and photos into a polished report with a checked species list, and the guide approves it before anyone sees it. It works for multi-day birding tours and for single-day outings such as whale watching in the Canary Islands. It is built as a real product, and there is a demo you can try in the browser.

Problem

Birding and wildlife tour companies send their guests a trip report: a daily narrative, a species list and photos. Guides usually write it by hand after a long day in the field, from memory and a scribbled checklist. It takes an hour that nobody has, species names drift between days, and the list is rarely checked against a standard taxonomy.

How it works

The guide spends a few minutes a day on input: notes typed or dictated the way they would tell a colleague, a rough species tally such as "40 griffons, lynx x1!, heard eagle owl, warbler?" and a few photos. On a boat with no signal the guide can record voice notes and positions; the phone keeps them and sends them when it is back in coverage. Each species name is checked against a standard taxonomy, which also fills in the scientific name, and a drafter writes the day's narrative from the guide's own notes and species list.

The output is a branded report with the operator's logo, colour and sign-off, a day-by-day species table, a photo gallery, reference photos of the species with their credits, and a numbered map of the sightings. It comes in English, Spanish, German or French. Guests open it through a private link the operator can revoke, it prints to PDF, and the sightings export as CSV.

Three rules

  • It never adds a species on its own. The narrative only mentions animals the guide actually listed. Anything uncertain (a question mark, "maybe", a name that isn't in the taxonomy) is flagged and waits for the guide.
  • The guide approves everything. Nothing reaches a client until the guide has resolved every flag and pressed approve.
  • The operator owns the data. Each approved report is also structured sighting data (species, count, place, day, observer) that is shared only with the operator's permission.

What the demo does

The demo above is the real workflow in miniature, in English and Spanish. A guide can dictate or type a day the way they would tell a colleague, with no format. The tool picks out the days, dates, times (including “half past six” and “a las siete menos cuarto”), weather, places, species and counts, plus behaviour, highlights and guests. It treats “didn't see it” as a miss rather than a sighting, and flags anything uncertain, heard-only or unknown for the guide to resolve. A live panel shows what it understood while the guide speaks.

It then drafts the narrative in three tones, builds the species table in English or Spanish with scientific names, adds the approval step, and exports the sightings as CSV or JSON.

Guide stats

Every approved report can be saved to the guide's logbook. The stats tab turns the logbook into weekly, monthly, yearly and all-time numbers: guiding days, guests, species, individuals, new species, species per day, the best day, how many sightings are backed by a photo, and the share of days on which each species was seen, which is an honest answer to “will we see it?”. A one-page card summarises it for clients and can be printed. The figures are self-reported by the guide, so in the product each one links back to the sightings and photos behind it.

The demo is a separate, browser-only version of the same idea, so it runs for free and uploads nothing: it matches about 390 species instead of checking the full GBIF taxonomy online, understands language with fixed rules, and writes the narrative from templates. Voice dictation uses the browser's own speech recognition where available. The demo's sample history is generated and clearly labelled as such.

The product behind the demo

Beyond the demo there is a working web app for operators, written in Python:

  • Accounts and teams. Sign-up with a free trial, guide invitations with seat limits, owner and guide roles, and pilot accounts managed from an admin page.
  • Tours and single outings. Multi-day trips with days, or an "outings" mode where a new outing is one tap and the last one can be repeated, for companies that sail every day.
  • Reading messy notes. It copes with typos, dictation errors ("links" for lynx), number words in English and Spanish, quick corrections ("3 no 4") and negations ("no lynx today"), and it learns each operator's own species.
  • Offline field notes. The app can be installed on a phone. Voice notes recorded with no coverage are turned into text with Whisper once the phone is back online, and GPS positions per sighting feed the report map.
  • Guests' QR page. A QR code per outing, or a permanent one for the boat, where guests leave their email and receive the report with Google and TripAdvisor review buttons. They can also upload photos, and the guide approves which ones appear, credited by name.
  • Approval and reports. A guide approves each day, and the report page shows what is still missing and any warnings from the fact-checks before it is sent.
  • Season insights. Target-species success rates, the best months for each species, website lines ready to copy and a season summary.
  • Billing and privacy. Stripe Checkout, the Customer Portal and webhooks, read-only mode when a trial ends unpaid, data export, account deletion and legal pages that cover guests, voice notes and positions.
  • A whale-watching example. A finished outing off La Palma and a year of outings with realistic seasonality, so a whale-watching operator sees their own world in the app. The interface is in English and Spanish.

Keeping the AI honest

A language model is good at reading messy notes and writing readable prose, but a report that invents an animal is worse than no report. So the AI never has the last word:

  • Species must be quoted. Every species the AI reads has to quote the guide's own words, or it is dropped.
  • Plain code checks the numbers and names. Counts must come from the guide's words, "no lynx" is never a sighting, and a question mark keeps a species unconfirmed. Species that the free parser read with certainty and the AI missed are added back.
  • The text is checked too. The draft cannot mention a species that is not on the list, a number that is not in the notes, invented weather, places, sounds or behaviour, or mix languages. A second model fact-checks the writer, and a failed draft gets a guided rewrite.
  • It is measured, not guessed. An admin page runs a set of messy example notes, each with the answer a careful human would give, against the live AI, so a change of model or prompt can be compared. Most of the project's recent work has been rounds of fixing what those tests found.

Business and data

The customer is the operator: a small monthly fee per company, fully remote, with no data licensing problem because the content comes from the operator. Plan prices are hidden while the app is in a pilot, and pilot companies can be given full access without an end date. The side effect is the interesting part: with permission, every report adds clean, expert-verified sighting records from many places at once, which is the dataset that trip and chance forecasting is usually missing.

How it is built

  • FastAPI, SQLAlchemy and Alembic with server-rendered Jinja pages and a little vanilla JavaScript. PostgreSQL in production, SQLite for local development.
  • Free hosting. The database is on Neon and the app on Render from a Blueprint file. Photos live in the database, so no file storage is needed. The free web service sleeps when idle, so the first visit after a while takes about 30 seconds.
  • Free AI. Any OpenAI-compatible provider works. The default is Groq's free tier with a separate model allowance for each job, and Cloudflare Workers AI takes the overflow. Whisper does the voice notes. Claude is an optional upgrade for the writing, and the product still works without any AI key, with a rule-based parser and drafter.
  • Open data. GBIF's backbone taxonomy for species names, free-licence Wikimedia Commons photos with author and licence shown, and OpenStreetMap with Leaflet for the map.
  • Email through Brevo, and Stripe in test mode with signed webhooks, ready to switch to live keys.
  • Security basics. Passwords hashed with scrypt, session cookies, CSRF protection, rate limits, security headers and photo location data removed.
  • 106 automated tests covering accounts, trips, the parser, the AI checks, guests, voice notes, species photos and billing.

Honest limits

This is a pilot-stage product with no paying customers yet, and billing runs in Stripe's test mode. The free AI tiers have daily limits, the free hosting is slow to wake up, and the free drafter writes from templates. The species parser and the fact-checks catch a lot but not everything, which is why the guide approves every day. The legal pages need a professional review before taking real customers. A WhatsApp or Telegram bot is still only an idea. The demo above is the in-browser version, so what it saves stays in your browser.
